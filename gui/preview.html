Five parts, and none of them optional

The skeleton

Every radar display that puts a human in the loop is built from the same five pieces. Systems that look nothing alike — a circle, a wedge, a rectangle — are the same skeleton with different flesh.

A picture

Raw energy drawn where it came from, so the operator can see things the computer has not decided are real. Never removed, however good the tracker gets.

Marks on top

The computer's opinion. Shape says what it is, colour says how you should feel about it, and the two always say the same thing twice.

A tag beside it

Three or four lines of text tied to the symbol by a thin line, so nobody has to look away from the picture to learn who something is.

A ruler

A movable line and a movable circle. The number that matters is often not one the designer thought to precompute.

A tripwire

A region the operator draws that shouts when something enters it. The operator sets the line, not the vendor.

Building the picture

Layers, decay and colour

The picture is three layers and the order never changes: a map underneath, the radar's energy in the middle, symbols on top. Each has its own brightness, and the top always wins.

The middle layer carries the best trick available. Keep a slow running average of what comes back from every direction and after a few turns it holds everything that isn't moving. Subtract it from the live return and what remains is only what changed. Draw the whole return in one colour and the changed part in a hotter one, in the same window, and the eye goes to the second colour with nobody having to decide what counts as a target.

Two colours, one window Everything in green · only what moved, in red

Map underneath

Dimmed hard, on its own brightness, never allowed to compete with the picture above it.

Everything, in green

The full return, cliff and buoy alike. The layer nobody is allowed to remove.

What moved, in red

Live minus its own running average. No threshold, no decision — just the part of the scene that was not there last time round.

Symbols on top

Drawn last, always opaque, never blended with the picture underneath.

Three ways to let the past fade The same scene, three decay settings

Smooth

Brightness bleeds away continuously. Set in seconds from full to black. Looks right, costs the most to compute.

Stepped

Each patch dims only when the beam next passes it. Set in number of sweeps, so the picture is tied to the aerial rather than the clock.

None

Nothing older than one sweep survives. The honest choice when a stale echo would be worse than a missing one.

Why it is a control

Long decay reveals a weak target by letting it build up. Short decay stops a busy sea filling the screen with ghosts. The operator picks.

What sits between the aerial and the glass

A dozen named stages, every one switchable, because which of them helps depends on the weather and on what you are looking for.

The chain Every stage optional · the order is the design

Walk it both ways

Turning a fan of returns into a square of pixels naively leaves holes at long range and throws data away close in. Iterate over both the samples and the pixels.

History survives the view

Changing range or panning must not wipe the trails. It ships as a named feature, which tells you how often it gets built the wrong way round.

Masks are polygons

An arbitrary outline, not a rectangle, because the thing you want to ignore is a headland or a building.

Record before the picture

Recording taps the radar's own coordinates upstream of everything, so replay runs the chain again and you can change your mind afterwards.

The radar layer belongs to a different program

Do not draw radar video inside your application. Run the scan conversion as a separate process, tell it which window to paint into, and send it short commands — this scale, this centre, this colour, this decay. A slow redraw in the interface then cannot stall the radar, and a flood of returns cannot freeze the buttons.

Four ways to draw the same energy

Presentations

The circular sweep is one option of several, and professional software routinely shows two or three at once from a single radar. Each throws away something different.

The same three targets, four presentations Live

Plan view

Direction as angle, distance as radius. Matches the world, wastes the corners, squashes everything close in.

Range vs direction

The same wedge unrolled into a rectangle. Uses every pixel, bends straight lines into curves.

Strength vs range

One direction, drawn as a graph. The only view that shows how strong a return is, which is how gain gets set honestly.

Range vs speed

The native output of a continuous-wave radar. A hovering drone and a moving car separate here even at the same distance.

Pointing the beam

And what it does to the screen

Three ways to aim a radar: turn the aerial with a motor, bolt it down and steer by shifting phase across a grid of small aerials, or do both at once. It sounds like a hardware detail. It is the single biggest influence on what the screen can be.

Watch the age counters below. A turning aerial resets all of them together, once a revolution, and nothing can be made fresher without spinning faster. A fixed array resets whichever ones it chooses, as often as it likes — so staleness stops being a property of the machine and becomes a budget the operator spends.

The same sky, three ways of looking at it Plan view · elevation · what it costs

Motor only

One beam, one height, one look per turn. The sweep line is where the metal is pointing and the fade behind it is genuinely how old the picture is.

Motor plus phase

The face turns but the beam need not follow. Stacked in elevation, so height arrives free; swung backwards against the rotation to linger where the weather is eating the signal.

Phase only

Four faces, nothing moving, every direction available every millisecond. Seams where the faces meet, and accuracy dips there.

What each one costs

Update rate, height, whether the energy can be aimed, and how gracefully it fails. Rarely the comparison in the brochure.

Rotation is a visible trade

Spin faster and the picture is fresher; spin faster and each patch of sky gets less energy, so range drops. The rate belongs on screen because it is a decision, not a specification.

Blanking sectors are drawn

Transmission stopped over particular bearings looks exactly like calm empty water unless it is marked.

Decay counted in sweeps

On a rotating set the natural unit of time is the revolution, so trails and integration are specified in sweeps. Change the rate and all of them move underneath you.

The operator gains a verb

With a motor, asking the radar to look somewhere means waiting. With an array you point and it looks there next — so the interface needs a cue action and somewhere to show a request is outstanding.

Coverage is not a clean wedge

Steer away from boresight and sensitivity falls off with the cosine of the angle until there is nothing left. A flat symmetrical fan overstates the edges every time.

Dead elements fade quietly

A motor stops and you notice. An array loses elements and keeps working with slightly less reach and worse sidelobes, announcing nothing. The count belongs on the health strip.

Why a long look matters

Digital beam forming floods the sector with one wide transmit beam and forms many receive beams at once, in software. Nothing scans, so the radar can dwell on a target for as long as it likes. Fine speed resolution needs a long look, and fine speed resolution is what separates a drone's spinning blades from a bird's wingbeat. A rotating radar gets tens of milliseconds per target per turn and can never have it.

Tracks, symbols and labels

The computer's opinion, drawn

A track is a claim: this is one object, it is here, it is going there. Everything about how it is drawn exists to let a person accept or reject that claim quickly.

Symbol, tag, trail, vector Every element of a track, and the states it moves between

Yours

You are responsible for it. The default state and the commonest colour on the screen.

Selected

Only ever one, and it changes appearance everywhere it appears — on the picture and in every list.

In conflict

Colour plus a filled panel plus a text alert. Three cues, because one is not enough for the thing that matters most.

Not yours

Present, someone else's problem. Dimmed rather than deleted, and filterable away entirely.

Three lines, no more

Identity on top, then two numbers that change — typically height and speed. Everything else is available on request. A tag that tries to say everything says nothing.

Shape is read before text

Eye-tracking work found the outline of a tag tells an operator whether it needs work before a single character is read. Boxing every tag made them easier to read and harder to find, which is a real measured trade.

Tags collide, so they move

Offer all three answers at once: drag a tag by hand, flip it to the other side, or run an algorithm that untangles the field. Operators use all three.

The trail is the speedometer

Three to five marks, equally spaced in time, so uneven spacing means acceleration and a bend means a turn. A human reads that instantly and a number slowly.

The vector is a promise

The stalk is where it will be, not where it is pointing — and the display must state the time span, because a prediction without its horizon is meaningless.

Trails must not look like targets

Written into standards as a sentence of its own. Where something has been can never be mistakable for where something is.

Telling things apart Shape and colour carrying the same message twice

Curves

Rounded outline, cyan. The category you are not worried about.

Corners

Pointed outline, red. The one you are.

Clipped corners

Yellow. Deliberately unlike both of the above — undecided is its own state, not a default.

Square

Green. Identified, present, not a party to anything.

A symbol whose colour and shape disagree is a bug, not a style choice.

Say it twice or don't say it

Roughly one person in twelve cannot rely on hue. Anything colour says must also be said by shape, position or a label — and then the display survives a failed colour channel too.

An identifier is a name

Every track gets a stable number the moment it exists, and that number is what people say to each other. Never re-use one: two people saying "track twelve" must mean the same twelve.

Density sets the technology

Ordinary vector graphics stall past one to two thousand symbols, a single drawing surface handles five to ten thousand, and only the graphics card gets you past fifty thousand.

Detection is measurably uneven

Trials find some symbol categories are spotted faster than others, and overlapping symbols wreck all of them. The picture is only as good as its least separable pair.

How the operator actually touches it

On-screen controls

This is the half that gets designed last and used most. A radar display is not read, it is operated — gain nudged, range stepped, a zone redrawn, a bad merge pulled apart — and the control vocabulary needed to do that is surprisingly small and surprisingly specific.

The governing idea: nothing should have to be found. A value you can see is a value you can change, by clicking the place it is displayed. The settings tree stops being somewhere you go.

The control vocabulary Eight patterns that cover almost everything

Coarse on click, fine on wheel

One control, two speeds, no mode to remember. Clicking steps the handful of values anyone actually uses; the wheel gives the full range.

Level and mode together

A slider shows how far it is turned up and whether a human or the machine is turning it. Half the trouble with an automatic control is not knowing it is on.

Buttons carry their value

A button labelled with its current setting rather than just its name turns a row of controls into a status panel you can also press.

Two routes to one number

Drag the handle on the picture or type the figure. Both must exist: one is fast, the other is exact, and operators use whichever suits the moment.

Show every option, grey the impossible

A setting displays its whole range of values with the live one boxed — a status display that happens to be clickable. What cannot be done now stays in place, greyed, so the operator learns it exists and that something is in the way.

Right-click the thing itself

A small mark on a readout means it has a menu behind it. To change how speed is measured, right-click the speed. Menus open beside the picture, never over it.

Say what the buttons do, now

A permanent line naming what left and right will do at this instant, switching mid-action to name the action and how to abandon it. The only modal-state indicator on the screen, and it is always visible.

Guard by delay, not by dialog

A confirmation box gets dismissed by reflex within a week. A control that must be held for two seconds, with the progress drawn on the control itself, does not — and it should still be undoable afterwards.

Regions are drawn, not configured

Guard zones and masks are arbitrary shapes pulled about by their corners, because what you want to ignore is a building or a headland.

Presets, and what they spare

A one-press reset returns range, rulers, vectors and alarm limits to known values — and explicitly does not drop the tracks or the map. It clears the last person's preferences, not the situation.

Some settings survive the reset

Gain and tuning are held over because they belong to the weather rather than to a person. Someone decided, line by line, which settings are opinions and which are measurements.

Keyboard reaches everything

Numbered menu items answer to their number, and the frequently used actions get a key. Under load, a hand that knows where to go beats a hand that has to look.

Menus, in place Right-click a readout · the menu opens beside the picture, never on it

All options, always visible

No dropdown, no dialog, no hidden state. The whole setting is legible without opening anything.

Greyed, not gone

Removing an unavailable item leaves the operator wondering whether the function exists at all.

The cursor changes shape

An arrow outside the picture, a cross inside it. A free reminder of which world your next click lands in.

Numbers reach the items

Nine items, each answering to its own key, so the same menu serves both the mouse and the muscle memory.

More than one sensor

Fusion, and how not to lie about it

A radar gives an excellent position and no idea who anything is. A transponder gives a name and lies if switched off. A camera gives certainty, in one direction. A direction finder gives a bearing and no range. Each is blind exactly where another sees.

The temptation is to average them into one confident number. Mature systems explicitly do not: they associate the reports, decide they are the same object, and then show one of them — and which one is a setting the operator owns. Nobody has ever measured the average of a radar position and a transponder position, so nobody displays it.

Four sensors, one picture What each track is made of · and where they disagree

R · radar

Where it is, to the metre, whether or not it wants to be seen. No idea what it is.

T · transponder

Name, type, destination, intent. Present only while it chooses to be.

C · camera

Certainty, in one direction at a time. Slaved to whichever track you select.

F · radio bearing

A line, not a point — and often a line to the operator rather than to the machine.

Association, and its undo How two reports become one track · and why a cross-fix is a patch

A score, not a verdict

Close enough for long enough gets a number. Showing that number is what lets an operator disagree with it.

One action pulls it apart

Written as a requirement. Automatic association without a manual undo is worse than no association at all.

Keep both sets of figures

Merging is a display decision, not a data decision. The underlying reports stay separate so the merge is always reversible.

A fix is a patch

Two bearings cross in an ellipse whose shape says more about the quality of the fix than any confidence number could.

Show the disagreement

The strongest pattern in service is a split symbol: one half is what your own sensor thinks, the other what the network thinks. When they differ you see it rather than having it resolved behind the scenes.

The gaps are the interesting part

A contact with a radar return and no transponder is the one worth looking at. A transponder with no radar return means something is wrong with the radar, the weather, or the story. Both deserve their own symbol.

Two symbols for one object

The dangerous failure, because nothing about it looks wrong. It doubles the apparent traffic and it happens whenever association is too timid.

Say what each track is made of

A row of source letters lit for the sensors currently contributing. It costs almost no space and it is the difference between trusting a symbol and knowing why.

The picture drives the camera

Select a track and the camera turns to it and keeps following. That link — from an abstract symbol to a live image — is what turns a plausible track into a decision someone will sign for.

Identity and position come from different places

Almost always. Design the track record so that each field remembers which sensor produced it, and the rest follows.

Listening instead of transmitting

The receiver's own display

A receiver that transmits nothing and listens to everyone else's radar answers a different question: who is looking at me, and how worried should I be. You get bearings without ranges and intent without positions, and the geometry of the display changes to suit.

Which produces the most counter-intuitive decision in the field, and it is correct: distance from the centre of the screen is not distance. It is how much trouble you are in.

Three receiver screens Who is looking · what they are · and what happens when they shout

Inner ring

Something has launched. The diamond marks whichever is worst, and making that judgement is the display's main job.

Middle ring

Locked on and tracking. Same emitter, different mode — and the change is what matters.

Outer ring

Emitting, sweeping, has not found you. Worth knowing, not yet a problem.

Strobe

Jamming denies range and leaves bearing. A line of noise with no distance on it, and the display has to say so rather than draw a target.

Sort by danger, not distance

Under threat an operator needs an order of business, not a map. Spending the whole screen on priority is the right trade — and it is the only display here that throws geometry away.

A bearing is a line

Drawing it as a point invents a range nobody measured. Draw the line, cross two of them for a position, and draw the patch where they cross.

Never one name when there are three

Identification is a library lookup against a crowded parameter space, so matches are routinely ambiguous. List the candidates with their confidences.

The change of mode is the event

The same transmitter searching, tracking and guiding is three situations from one signal. The alert belongs on the transition, not the existence.

Sort the pulses on screen

What arrives is every emitter interleaved. Showing the sorted lanes, one per emitter with its pulse spacing labelled, is most of the value the display adds.

Jamming is drawn, not hidden

A bright wedge along that bearing, labelled, plus the range at which detection is expected to return. Quietly showing a clean picture with a hole in it is far worse.

Where you cannot see

Coverage, terrain and shadows

Ground does three separate jobs. It blocks — a ridge casts a shadow no transmitter power fixes. It constrains — a vehicle is on a road, at the height the ground happens to be. And it locates — the shape of the ground beneath you is unique enough to tell you where you are with no satellite involved.

What the hill takes away Side view above · the same coverage from overhead below

Seen

Line of sight from the aerial, with the earth's curve included. Computed from an elevation model, not guessed.

Shadow

Behind a ridge, below the horizon, or under the beam. Drawn, because an unmarked gap looks exactly like empty sky.

Coasted

A track crossing a shadow is predicted, not observed. Dashed, and labelled with how long it has been running on faith.

Visible is not detectable

A coverage map says a target could be seen. Whether it would be depends on its size and the weather, and the honest tools say which question they answer.

Coverage answers "visible to what"

Change the target height and the picture changes, so the answer is always relative to something. Earth curvature puts a floor under long range regardless of terrain.

The map is a tracker input

A tracker that knows the road network keeps motion models matching nearby roads and makes soft rather than hard decisions about which one. Better prediction, easier matching, fewer impossible tracks.

Height is not free either

A ground vehicle is at the ground's height and its velocity lies along the local slope. Constrain the filter to the terrain — softly, so an inaccurate map does not wreck it.

Terrain as a position fix

Measure your height above the ground as you move and you collect a profile that fits the stored map in essentially one place. That is how navigation works when the satellites are jammed.

A gap you have drawn is coverage information. A gap you have not drawn is a lie.

When an input dies

The part with no equivalent in ordinary software

Every number carries the instrument that produced it. When that instrument is not the proper one, the label turns yellow. When it stops answering, the number is replaced with asterisks — never left frozen at its last good value, which is the most dangerous state a readout can be in.

Then it gets stricter. Lose the compass and the display drops to the one orientation that needs no compass and erases every track, chart and map that was drawn using it. A blank screen you can see is blank beats a confident picture that is quietly wrong.

Four states of the same panel Healthy · fallback source · signal lost · compass lost

Never a stale number

Thirty seconds without a signal and the value becomes asterisks. A number that has stopped changing looks exactly like one that is correct.

Failover announces itself

The system switches source by itself and raises an alert saying it did. Silent recovery is how an operator ends up trusting a figure from somewhere else.

The label names the number

The same field reads ground track, water track or plain course depending on what feeds it, so the number cannot be misread.

Identifiers are never recycled

A vacated track number is not handed to a new target until restart.

A pulse you can watch

A small ring that turns while the software is alive. Its one job is to stop — because a frozen picture looks entirely normal, and every other indicator is part of the thing that has stopped.

And a watchdog that shouts

Something outside the drawing loop notices the freeze within thirty seconds and makes a noise of its own.

Machine-chosen values look different

Tie the trail length to the range scale and the number turns yellow. Anything the system is choosing on your behalf is coloured unlike anything you chose.

Draw the edge of what you can see

A separate marker for how far returns actually reach, not the range you zoomed to. Two different numbers, and only one is a promise about coverage.

Alerting

Priority, rhythm and budget

Alert design is where good displays are most often ruined. The pattern that works fixes the rhythm by urgency and leaves pitch and tone colour free, so one learned rhythm means the same thing forever while the voice identifies which part of the system is speaking.

The cues, drawn Alert rhythms above · what a moving thing actually sounds like below

Highest priority

Three short signals every 7 to 10 seconds. Immediate threat, immediate action.

Middle

Two short signals, every 15 seconds to 5 minutes. Needs attention, not this second. A different rhythm, not a different volume.

Lowest

Silence, deliberately. It goes on the list and waits to be read.

And the raw sound

Unprocessed Doppler is already audible. A drone's blade buzz around a quiet body is the whole reason it can be told from a bird by ear.

Only the worst thing sounds

With two alerts unacknowledged you hear the higher one. Two alert sounds at once is not twice the information, it is none.

Acknowledging changes state

Unacknowledged flashes; acknowledged goes solid and stays until the situation itself has passed. The operator can quieten the screen without losing the fact.

Silence is timed and short

Silencing buys tens of seconds, not permission. New alerts sound through it and nothing is dismissed — only quietened.

Ignoring it escalates

An unacknowledged warning repeats, and can be promoted to the level above. Silence is not treated as consent.

Count what you emit

About one alarm a minute is the human ceiling. Fewer than six an hour is the target, ten in ten minutes is a flood. Past that people stop listening, and that is a design failure.

Most alerts need no action

Studies of deployed alerting found the large majority required nothing from the operator. Every one of those is training them to ignore the rest.

Interface sound is separate

The click a control makes has its own volume, kept apart from the alerts. Turning down the chatter must never turn down the warnings.

Give the ear its own channel

The Doppler shift is already an audible tone. Piping it to a speaker gives a classifier that needs no training data and never goes down.

Six, not eleven

Having eleven approved colours does not mean using them. Guidance caps colour coding at six on one screen, because recognition accuracy falls off a cliff beyond that.

Three to one, minimum

A floor on how much brighter a symbol must be than what is behind it — and a warning to the operator when their own brightness control takes them below it. They may make it worse, but not silently.

Red means now

Red for act immediately, amber for act soon, anything but red or green for information. Every decorative red makes the real one worth less.

Pairs that fail

Saturated red beside blue, red on black, yellow on white, yellow on green, blue for anything small. Several are what an untrained eye reaches for first.

A dark mode built by people who meant it

A light switch and a dark switch is not the answer. Mature displays offer eight named palettes — three light levels crossed with three background tints — and then, underneath, fourteen independent brightness sliders, one per class of thing on the screen. The heading line has its own. The coordinate grid has its own.

Why that many

At night the echoes want to be dim and the tags must stay readable. One master brightness cannot serve both, so give the individual controls.

Colour by strength

Two echo palettes run red for a strong return through to green for a weak one, so a cliff stops looking like a dinghy without anyone classifying either.

Three light levels, not two

Day, dusk, night. Dusk is the one people leave out and the one a watch spends hours in.

Text has a size floor

Minimum character height and width published against the viewing distance the screen is designed for. Legibility in millimetres, not left to whoever picks the font.

Time-bounded honesty

The rules underneath

What transfers

Say it twice or don't say it

Anything colour tells the operator must also be told by shape, position or a label. It is what keeps the display working for the tenth of people who cannot rely on hue — and for the day a colour channel fails.

Never delete the raw picture

Draw detections underneath the conclusions. Operators are trained to catch the target the tracker dropped, and a display that only shows tracks hides its own failures.

One selection, everywhere

At most one thing is selected across the whole interface, and picking it anywhere lights it up everywhere. This single rule is what stops a multi-panel display fragmenting into several unrelated screens.

Show where it has been

Three to five fading marks, equally spaced in time. Humans read speed and turn from a trail instantly and from a number slowly — and the trail must never look like a target.

Predict, and say how far ahead

The stalk is where it will be, with the operator choosing the horizon. The prediction is worthless unless the screen states the time span it covers.

Let them measure by hand

A draggable bearing line and a draggable range circle. The operator will always want a number nobody thought to precompute, and the alternative is that they guess.

Every readout is its own settings button

A mark on a box means it has a menu behind it. You change how a value is measured by clicking the value. Nothing has to be found.

Show every option, grey the impossible ones

A setting displays its whole range with the live one boxed — a status display that happens to be clickable. What cannot be done stays in place, greyed, so the operator learns it exists.

Say what the buttons do, right now

A permanent line naming both button actions, changing mid-action to name the action and the way out. The display explains itself continuously instead of relying on a manual.

Coarse on the click, fine on the wheel

Clicking steps through the values anyone actually uses; the wheel gives the full range at fine resolution. One control, two speeds, no mode to remember.

Guard by delay, not by dialog

A confirmation box is dismissed by reflex within a week. A control held down for two seconds, with the progress drawn on the control, is not — and it should still be undoable.

Two routes to every number

Drag it on the picture or type it in a field. One is fast and one is exact, and which is wanted changes minute by minute.

Sleep is a third state

Between fully live and deleted sits quiet. Two states force the operator to choose between noise and blindness; three do not.

Never freeze a dead number

Thirty seconds without a signal and the value is replaced by asterisks. A figure that has stopped updating is indistinguishable from one that is correct.

Delete what depended on the dead sensor

Lose the reference and erase everything drawn from it, then force the display into a mode that does not need it. A blank screen you can see is blank beats a confident picture that is wrong.

Say which sensor said so

Beside every number, the source that produced it. When two numbers disagree the operator needs to know which instrument to distrust, and that is not something to go hunting for.

The field name says what kind of number it is

Change the source and the label changes with it, so the number underneath cannot be read as something it is not.

Never re-use an identifier

A vacated number is not handed to a new object until restart. Two people saying "track twelve" must mean the same twelve.

Associate, do not average

Decide two reports are the same object, keep both sets of figures, and display whichever the operator asked for. Nobody measured the average.

Breaking a merge is one action

Automatic association without a manual undo is worse than no association at all.

Sort by danger, not by distance

When the question is "how much trouble am I in", geometry is the wrong answer. Spend the screen on priority instead.

A bearing is a line, not a dot

Drawing a bearing as a point invents a range nobody measured. Cross two lines for a position, and draw the patch where they cross.

Never one name when the library gives three

Identification against a crowded parameter space is routinely ambiguous. List the candidates with confidences; a single confident name hides the doubt.

The change of mode is the event

Alert on the transition, not the existence. The thing has been there for twenty minutes; what changed is that it started tracking.

Draw the shadow, not just the coverage

Compute where the ground blocks the beam, allow for the earth's curve, and paint the blind regions. An unmarked gap is indistinguishable from empty sky.

Predicted must not look observed

A track crossing a blind region is a guess. Draw it differently, label how long it has been coasting, and let it die on a stated timer.

Say whether you mean visible or detectable

A coverage map says a target could be seen. Whether it would be detected depends on its size and the weather. State which question you are answering.

Draw the edge of what you can actually see

A separate marker for how far returns genuinely reach, not the range you zoomed to. Only one of those is a promise about coverage.

The state of the machine is on the glass

Separate indicators for the failures that look identical from the picture: am I receiving, is the aerial turning, am I keeping up. Each with a few seconds of hysteresis so a blanked sector does not cry wolf.

A pulse you can watch, and a watchdog that shouts

A ring that turns while the software is alive, and something outside the drawing loop that notices when it stops.

Machine-chosen values look different

Anything the system is deciding on the operator's behalf is coloured unlike anything the operator decided.

Declutter is a control — until it isn't

Give the operator the knife. Then note what a mature system does when a danger alarm fires: it switches the hidden layer back on by itself.

Rhythm for urgency, timbre for identity

Fix the pattern by how bad it is and leave pitch and tone colour free, so one learned rhythm means the same thing forever while the voice says which box is speaking.

One sound at a time, and count them

Only the highest unacknowledged priority makes noise. Then budget: about one a minute is the human ceiling, and past it people stop listening.

Alarms change state, not existence

Acknowledging stops the flashing and leaves it solid until the situation itself has passed. Quieten the screen without losing the fact.

Design for the dark

Three light levels, per-element brightness, and no hue shift as it dims — because if the colours move, the colour code stops meaning anything.

Putting it together

A small forward-looking radar

The parts above, assembled for a compact radar that watches a forward sector rather than spinning through a full circle. Two things drive the layout: the picture is a wedge, not a disc, and the radar's natural output is a distance-versus-speed grid rather than a set of blips — so that grid is a first-class panel, not a hidden debugging view.

The third panel earns its place. A rolling trace of how a target's speed wobbles: spinning blades write a fuzzy band, a wingbeat writes a slow ripple, a wheeled vehicle writes a clean line. It is the difference between an alarm the operator believes and one they learn to ignore.

This last one is not a drawing. Click a track in the wedge or in the list and it lights up in all four panels at once. Drag across the picture to lay a bearing line and a range ring exactly where the question is. Drag the guard box somewhere it will catch something. Right-click to ask the array to spend longer on one bearing. The range steps on a click and trims on the wheel from the same control, and holding the alarm down acknowledges it — which changes what it looks like without making it go away. Keys 1 to 4 pick a track.

The control on the far right takes the timing reference away. Watch what the display does about it. The bearing scale turns yellow, because bearing was the thing that reference was holding up. The calibration figure becomes *.* instead of the last good number. Every track starts coasting on its last known heading with a countdown against it, and eight seconds later they die rather than quietly persisting. The speed trace stops writing and says that it stopped. Restore it and the targets come back under new numbers, because the old ones are spent. That single control is the argument of this whole page.

The wedge, not a circle

Drawing an unfilled disc around a radar that only looks forward claims cover it does not have. The dark area outside the wedge is information.

Health as a strip, always

Transmit state, gain, temperature, timing lock, and how long since calibration. A drifted array lies about direction while looking perfectly healthy.

Ranges that are round numbers

Fixed steps, always labelled, never a free zoom. Operators build a mental sense of scale that continuous zoom destroys.

One click, four panels

Selecting a track anywhere highlights it everywhere and pins its speed signature. Without that link, four panels are four separate problems.

Two colours in the wedge

The whole return dim, and the part that changed since the running average in red on top — separating a hovering target from the hedge behind it before any tracker commits.

Compute the mask, don't draw it

Feed it building heights and run line-of-sight from the aerial's real position, and the blind regions draw themselves — and correct themselves when the radar is moved.

A listen button on the selected track

Play the selected target's own Doppler tone. It costs nothing, needs no model, and a person who has heard twenty will beat a classifier on the twenty-first.

An alarm budget, written down

Decide how many times an hour this is allowed to make a noise, and treat exceeding it as a bug. Something that cries wolf gets muted within a week.
